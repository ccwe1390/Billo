HackTN26 Health Access Plan
Oct 17, 2026 · @Someone
The decision
Build [Name] (unnamed for now): a photo of a Nashville hospital bill goes in, and that hospital's financial assistance application comes out, pre-filled, checked against its published policy, with every step explained in Arabic or Spanish for the family member helping a parent.
The decisive reason: this is the point in the bill journey where help already exists and goes unclaimed. In Dollar For's research, only 29% of patients with hospital bills they can't afford learn about, apply for, and receive charity care, and 52% said their hospital told them nothing about it. Our product doesn't invent a benefit; it gets families to one they are already entitled to apply for.
What changed from the original idea. The bill explainer and error checker become step one, not the product. Three reasons: error-checking apps are crowded; comparing a bill to an insurance EOB doesn't help uninsured families, who get no EOB; and a general assistant already explains bills well.
"Can't people just ask Claude?" For explaining a bill, mostly yes, and judges will think that. What a general assistant does not do on its own is the last mile: it answers the question asked, and families who have never heard of hospital financial assistance don't ask about it. It also doesn't hand over one hospital's application, filled in, with the deadline and the documents that hospital requires. That is a hypothesis until we test it (see Validation, task 1). If a general assistant does all of it reliably, our pitch has to narrow to Arabic access and the family workflow.
Languages: Spanish and Arabic only for the demo. These are Nashville's top two non-English languages and both are supported by Oracle's translation service. Kurdish is third in Metro schools and is not supported, which we state as a known gap.

Assumptions we're making, and what they change:
• Team of 4, two people who can code. With only one coder, drop the database and the Arabic layout polish first.
• OCI Generative AI works in your tenancy (US Midwest, Chicago). Verify in the first 30 minutes by calling one model. If it fails, see failure mode 1.

• The only reachable users are people at the event. Hospital offices and community organizations are closed this weekend, so validation is designed around attendees and mentors.
The problem
Families pay or ignore hospital bills that the hospital's own financial assistance policy would reduce or erase, because nobody connects the bill to the program.
The moment (illustrative persona, not an interview): Leila, 24, reads mail for her father, who speaks Arabic, has no insurance, and works for cash. He went to a Nashville emergency department and a statement for several thousand dollars arrives. He wants to set up a payment plan. Neither of them knows the hospital has a program for people at his income, or that there is an application window.
Documented evidence
• Help goes unclaimed nationally. A Dollar For study estimated nonprofit hospitals fail to deliver at least $14 billion a year in charity care to eligible patients. In its survey of over 1,600 patients, only 29% with unaffordable bills learned about, applied for, and received it; 52% got no information from their hospital; 45% of applicants got no help applying. The most common denial reasons were an old bill or paperwork problems. (ClearHealthCosts summary)
• Help works when someone assists. The same study found approval rates were highest, 67%, when patients got help from Dollar For or the hospital directly.
• Tennessee has more people who depend on it. Tennessee has not adopted Medicaid expansion as of May 2026 (KFF), and about 95,000 uninsured adults sit in the coverage gap (CBPP).
• Tennessee law gives uninsured patients specific protections. All hospitals must post a statement of their charity care policies, and may not charge an uninsured patient more than 175% of cost (Hilltop Institute).
• The rules are real and specific. VUMC bases assistance on adjusted gross income for the current or prior year against the Federal Poverty Guidelines, on a sliding scale, and decides within 30 days of an application (VUMC summary). VUMC's billed charges for assistance-eligible patients are capped at the amount generally billed, 24% of charges as of April 30, 2026 (VUMC).
• Nashville's language mix points to Spanish and Arabic. Metro's Office of New Americans named Spanish, Arabic, Kurdish, Somali, Swahili, Burmese, Amharic, Vietnamese, Nepali and Farsi as the top 10 languages (Nashville Scene); the top three in Metro schools are Spanish, Arabic and Kurdish (WKRN).
• Even the published summaries disagree. Today, one CareRoute page lists VUMC free care up to 300% of poverty, while CareRoute's own VUMC guide and HealthBillCentral say 200%. Someone has to own the source of truth; that becomes part of our pitch.
Hypotheses we have not shown yet (validation tests these)
• Adult children are often the ones handling a parent's bills.
• These families don't know the program exists when the bill arrives.
• Paperwork is a bigger barrier than eligibility, especially proof of income for people paid in cash.
• VUMC already publishes its policy in Arabic (PDF), so translation alone is not the gap. Connecting a specific bill to a specific action is.

Product definition: [Name] turns a photo of a Nashville hospital bill into that hospital's financial assistance packet (eligibility estimate, pre-filled application fields, document checklist, deadline and a short cover letter), explained in Arabic or Spanish, for the family member helping a parent.
Why it's the strongest choice under our constraints: it ends in a completed action rather than an explanation; its rules come from one public document we can verify today; and it can be shown end to end in under two minutes with synthetic data.
Most important untested assumption: that families who would qualify don't find out about the program when the bill arrives, and that a packet triggered by the bill gets them further than what they do today, including asking a general AI assistant.
Evidence that would make us narrow or abandon it:
• If general assistants, given only the bill, reliably raise financial assistance, state VUMC's limits correctly and list the documents (validation task 1), drop the eligibility claim and pitch only the Arabic packet and the family workflow.
• If interviewees say the hospital already screened them automatically or they all knew about the program, narrow to the real blocker they name, most likely proof of income for cash workers.
Fallback if we can't confirm VUMC's thresholds from VUMC's own PDF by 3 pm: encode a clearly labeled synthetic hospital ("Cumberland General, synthetic") with rules modeled on the same structure. The flow, the packet and the demo stay identical.
Critique and revision. The first version of this plan was: explain the bill in five languages, check for errors, compare the bill to the EOB, check eligibility at three Nashville hospitals, and write a letter. Critique: four features means none works convincingly in a two minute demo; the EOB comparison doesn't apply to uninsured families; three hospitals triples the policy checking, and today's aggregator data already disagrees; Chinese, Vietnamese and Hindi aren't Nashville's main needs; and "we apply for you" would overclaim and copy CareRoute. Revised: one hospital verified from its own document, two languages, one action, every rule shown with its source and date, error checking deferred, and a hand-off to the hospital's counselors or Dollar For instead of applying on anyone's behalf.
The smallest impressive product
One journey must work live: synthetic bill photo, confirmed fields, a cited policy result, and a finished packet, in under 90 seconds.
The five screens
1. Start. Opened by scanning a QR code printed on the hospital's statement, so the hospital is already selected (or from a community group's link). Pick a language (العربية, Español, English) and "This bill is for: me / a family member." One line: "Nothing is saved unless you choose to. Demo uses synthetic bills only."
2. Bill. Upload or photograph the bill. The extracted fields appear beside the image: hospital, statement date, account number, amount due, date of service, insurance status. Each field is editable and must be confirmed. Anything unreadable is left blank and asked for, never guessed.
3. Three questions. Household size; yearly household income; "Do you file a tax return?" (changes the document list). No questions about immigration status, Social Security number or ID.
4. Result, in the chosen language with an English toggle. Example: "A household of 4 with $38,000 a year is at 115% of the 2026 federal poverty guideline. VUMC's policy (effective date, page number, link) lists this income range as [tier from Appendix B]. The hospital makes the final decision." Below it: the application deadline, three lines on what the bill is, and a card on Tennessee's protections for uninsured patients.
5. Packet. The application fields pre-filled from the bill and the answers; the document checklist from the policy; a short English cover letter asking VUMC to review the account for financial assistance, with an Arabic or Spanish translation beside it; and where to send it (VUMC lists financial.assistance@vumc.org). Buttons: Download PDF, and "Share with a hospital counselor or Dollar For."

Grounding and safety rules in code
• Extraction returns strict JSON with a confidence per field; a missing field is null and the screen asks for it.
• The engine's output (numbers, tier, deadline, documents, citations) is the only thing the language model sees. After generation, every number in the text is checked against the engine's numbers; any mismatch falls back to a fixed template.
• The user confirms every field; the packet is a draft they choose to send; the result always says the hospital decides.
• The Tennessee protections card is fixed text with its source and a line saying it is information, not legal advice.
Endpoints (all stateless): POST /extract (image to fields), POST /evaluate (fields and answers to result with citations), POST /explain (result and language to text), POST /packet (everything to PDF).
Tables: policy_rules (hospital, rule, value, source link, page, effective date, verified by, review date); events (date, hospital, language, step reached, result tier, packet made; no personal fields); saved_bills (opt in only, inside the partner's login; seeded for the demo). Two more endpoints: GET /dashboard (counts) and POST /reminders (computed dates; preview only this weekend).
Privacy and HIPAA
• In a pilot, the tool runs inside the partner's existing login (the hospital's patient portal or the community group's account). We don't build accounts.
• If a hospital deploys it, we act as its vendor under a HIPAA business associate agreement. Before a pilot, confirm which Oracle services are covered by Oracle's HIPAA program; don't claim any until checked.
• Nothing is saved unless the family opts in, and partners see only anonymous counts. No data passes between patients: each request carries only one family's data, with no shared chat history, no saved records, no cache, and no logging of bill contents.
• Test it: run two synthetic families back to back and confirm nothing from the first appears in the second's screens, model prompts or logs.

Two minute pitch

Nashville's big hospital systems all publish programs that cut or erase bills for low-income patients. But nationally, only 29% of patients with bills they can't afford learn about this help, apply, and get it. More than half say their hospital never told them.
Often the person who opens that bill is a daughter reading mail for a parent who speaks Spanish or Arabic, Nashville's top two languages after English. She sees a balance and a phone number, not the program, the deadline, or the forms.
Any chatbot can explain a bill. Nobody hands you the next step. [Name] does. The hospital prints a code on its statement. She scans it and photographs the bill. The AI reads and translates; it never decides. A rules engine applies the hospital's rules, and the hospital makes the final call.

Ten judge questions
Answer in two or three sentences, lead with the fact, and say "we don't know yet" when that's true.
1. "Why can't people just ask ChatGPT or Claude?" "We tested that with this exact bill: [task 1 result]. Chatbots explain bills well; what they don't do on their own is apply one hospital's current rules with a citation and hand you the finished application. Our AI doesn't decide anything; a rules engine does."
2. "CareRoute already does this in Nashville. How are you different?" "CareRoute is a paid service that negotiates for you, in English and Spanish. We're free, we start from the hospital's own statement, we work in Arabic, and the family stays in control. We'd rather hand off to the hospital's counselors or Dollar For than act on anyone's behalf. Today, CareRoute's own pages also disagree about VUMC's free care limit, which is why every rule we show carries its source and date."
3. "Policies change. Who keeps the rules right?" "Each rule is a database row with its source document, page, effective date, and who verified it. In a pilot, the hospital's financial assistance office or the community partner signs off, the poverty table updates every January, and any rule past its review date is flagged instead of shown."
4. "What if the AI misreads the bill or mistranslates?" "People confirm every field before it's used. Numbers and dates are locked, and every number in the Arabic or Spanish text is checked against the engine; a mismatch falls back to a fixed template. On our 10 synthetic test bills, it read [x] of 10 correctly."
5. "You're handling families' bills and income. What about privacy and HIPAA?" "In a pilot it runs inside the partner's existing login, and if a hospital deploys it, we'd be its vendor under a business associate agreement. Each request carries only one family's data: no shared chat history, no saved records, no cache, no logs of bill contents, so nothing can pass between patients. We never ask about immigration status, Social Security numbers or ID, and this weekend we used only synthetic bills. Before a pilot, we'd confirm which Oracle services are covered by Oracle's HIPAA program."
6. "What about for-profit hospitals like HCA, which aren't under the nonprofit rules?" "The registry is per hospital, so adding one is verifying its own published policy, not new code. Tennessee requires all hospitals to post their charity care policies and limits what uninsured patients can be charged. We'd encode HCA's policy from HCA's own documents, not from third-party summaries."
7. "Who pays for this?" "Never families. Community groups pay a flat yearly fee or use grants; hospitals pay per completed application, not per scan, so we only earn when a family finishes the step that matters. Nonprofit hospitals must try to work out eligibility before serious collection steps, and Dollar For estimates about $7 billion a year in eligible bills ends up as bad debt. If a hospital pays, the family still sees the same cited result from the hospital's own published policy. The pilot itself would be free."
8. "How do you know it helps real people?" "We don't yet. We measured understanding in a small synthetic test and heard from [N] people who've handled a family bill. The pilot metric is the share of eligible families whose packet becomes a completed application within the deadline."
9. "Is this built into an existing system, or is it a new app?" "It starts from something every patient already gets: the hospital's statement. The hospital prints a QR code that opens our tool with that hospital selected. The next step is a link inside the hospital's patient portal, using the login patients already have."
10. "Why not sell through insurance companies?" "For uninsured patients there's no insurer, so this version is for the provider side. Insurers matter later for people with high deductibles, where comparing the bill to the insurance summary could come back."
